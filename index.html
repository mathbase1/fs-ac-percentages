A QUICK CALCULATOR METHOD

Keep the whole in mind.

The original amount is 100%.
A multiplier is the decimal you multiply the original amount by.

These three examples all start with £80, but they ask for different things.

Find 20% of £80

20 ÷ 100 = 0.20

80 × 0.20 = 16

The part is £16.

Increase £80 by 20%

100% + 20% = 120%

80 × 1.20 = 96

The new total is £96.

Decrease £80 by 20%

100% − 20% = 80%

80 × 0.80 = 64

The amount left is £64.

For an increase: 120 ÷ 100 = 1.20.
For a decrease: 80 ÷ 100 = 0.80.
0.20 and 0.2 mean the same thing.

Both methods are welcome.

Teacher view

Deliberate Level 1 boundary

This independent trainer focuses on Pearson Edexcel Level 1 content 14, supported by decimal equivalence in content 16 and discount calculations in content 19.
All stated percentage rates are multiples of 5%.
Money and measurement calculations produce exact whole numbers or terminating decimals with at most two decimal places.
Counts of people or items always have whole-number answers.

Challenge comes from distinguishing a change amount from a final total, selecting data from tables, comparing offers and carrying out straightforward connected steps.
There are no reverse percentages, unknown percentage-change calculations, compound interest, repeated percentage changes or algebraic growth formulae.
Simple interest is Level 1 content, but is deliberately outside this focused percentage trainer.

What changes adaptively

There are separate learning records for finding the percentage amount, finding an increased total and finding a reduced or remaining total.
Three consecutive independent successes at the current working level move that skill to more demanding wording or connected steps.
Two supported or unsuccessful outcomes in the last three current-level checks move it back for consolidation.
Related questions return after a correction or support.
A skill that has not appeared in the previous six questions is prioritised.
Occasional easier questions revisit secure skills without counting towards promotion.

The first six questions are gentle non-calculator percentage amounts.
A struggling learner gets more halves and tenths rather than being pushed through a fixed increase in difficulty.
After those six, the integrated calculator becomes available for everyone and a short explanation introduces multipliers.
Calculator use is never treated as a weakness in the calculator-allowed questions.
Speed does not affect adaptation.

Support and points

10 points: a whole question correct on the first check without a hint or example.
5 points: correct after a retry without other support.
3 points: correct with a hint or example.
0 points: solution revealed or question unfinished.
Correct parts remain locked when another part needs a retry.
Partial successes are included in the detailed review, but a whole-question point award requires all parts to be correct.

Answers are marked exactly, not with the ±£1 / ±1 tolerance in the supplied GCSE template.
For example, 18, 18.0 and 18.00 are equivalent.
An incorrect value one penny away is not evidence of mastery.
This is answer checking for practice, not a reproduction of Pearson's method-mark scheme.
Teacher review of paper working is still needed.

What this is not

This is a focused adaptive practice tool, not a complete examination simulator, an official Pearson resource or a validated prediction of exam readiness.
The six-question start is a teaching choice, not the structure or weighting of a Pearson examination.
Multiplier prompts and the help tools are teaching scaffolds, not a claim that every prompt reproduces an exam question format.
